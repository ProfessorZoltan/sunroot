# Regenerative & Synergistic Systems Across Cultures and Biomes

Oct 5, 2026 · @Eric

Across every major biome, the most durable food and settlement systems share one trait: almost nothing is waste, because each component's by-product is another component's feed, fertilizer, fuel, shade or habitat. This doc surveys about 40 such systems, past and present, grouped by biome.

Each system is read through two lenses:

- **Output → input loops:** a by-product of one element (manure, mud, leaf litter, sewage, heat) becomes a resource for another.
- **Beneficial proximity:** placing two things side by side helps both (mutualism) or helps one without harming the other (commensalism), for example shade, wind shelter, pest control or shared warmth.

In the tables, loops are written as chains with arrows (silkworm frass → fish → pond mud → mulberry). Sources sit in their own column. A closing section pulls out the design patterns that recur across cultures.

The common weakness is labour: each loop depends on people moving mud, feed and manure by hand, which is why these systems fade when wages rise and revive when chemical inputs become costly or distrusted.

## Temperate woodlands and grasslands

In temperate zones the standard pattern is a woodland-to-field transfer: trees, heath or grass outside the village are harvested (as leaves, sods, acorns or grazing) and their nutrients are moved, often through animals, onto cropland near homes.

The Hawaiian ahupuaʻa is the clearest whole-watershed design in this survey: every loop is placed in the order water flows, so the waste of each stage is already in position to feed the next.

## Patterns that recur across cultures

Cultures that never met arrived at the same handful of design moves. Each pattern below lists where it appears in this doc.

&#91;embedded content: mulberry-dyke fish pond loop · 4 elements, 2 products sold\]

The Pearl River Delta's mulberry-dyke pond is the archetype: each element's leftovers are the next one's feed, and only cocoons and fish leave the loop.

| Pattern                            | How it works                                                                                            | Systems that use it                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Waste becomes feed                 | A by-product of one element is the main input of the next, so nothing leaves the farm as waste          | Mulberry-dyke ponds, VAC, pokkali rice-prawn, four-in-one greenhouse, Edo night soil, sewage-fed bheris |
| Animals as nutrient collectors     | Animals graze or forage over a wide area, then are concentrated where their dung is wanted              | Pigeon towers, boma glades, plaggen stables, dehesa pigs, Alpine transhumance, salmon carried by bears  |
| Mud and sediment lifted back       | Nutrients that wash downhill into water are dredged out and returned to land                            | Chinampas, mulberry dykes, VAC ponds, waru waru canals                                                  |
| Stacking in space                  | Tall plants shelter shorter ones, so several crops share one plot and one water supply                  | Oasis three-tier gardens, Chagga and Kandyan gardens, Three Sisters, dehesa                             |
| Staggering in time                 | Rotations and successions let one crop's leftovers feed the next, or starve pests between crops         | Pokkali (rice then prawns), Maya milpa cycle, satoyama coppice, Bali subak fallows                      |
| Water as the conveyor              | Homes, fields and ponds are placed in the order water flows, so each stage's runoff feeds the one below | Hawaiian ahupuaʻa, Hani terraces, Ifugao terraces, subak, qanat oases                                   |
| Protect the source above           | Forest or pasture upslope is kept intact because it supplies water and soil to everything below         | Hani water forests, Ifugao muyong, subak crater-lake forests                                            |
| Share heat                         | Animals, cooking fires or water bodies placed next to people or crops pass their warmth along           | Byre-houses, kang and ondol, waru waru canals, four-in-one greenhouse                                   |
| Rules and rituals hold it together | Shared calendars, guardians and contracts stop any one user from breaking the loop                      | Bali water temples, Hani water and forest guards, Edo night-soil contracts, Gunditjmara eel traps       |

Three failure modes recur just as often:

1. **One-way transfers.** When the outfield is harvested faster than it regrows, the loop becomes extraction (heath sods in plaggen farming).
2. **Labour and price.** Hand-carried mud, dung and leaves lose out when wages rise or chemical fertilizer is cheap (mulberry dykes, pigeon towers, satoyama).
3. **Breaking the upstream link.** Clearing the forest above terraces, damming a river or diverting a city's sewage starves every element below (Zuni waffle gardens after 1908, East Kolkata today).
